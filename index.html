1What the Digital Corridors Initiative actually is

DCI is a federally assisted, state-led effort run out of OST-R (Seval Oz) jointly with FHWA, with AASHTO, TRB, and ITS America, to modernize the interstates through large-scale deployment of "Transportation Digital Infrastructure." Its first use case is real-time work-zone and lane-closure data, end-to-end across interstate corridors. It rolled out across 2026 rather than launching on one date:

  • Feb 2026 — OST-R Request for Information on a National Strategy for Transportation Digital Infrastructure (docket DOT-OST-2026-0430); ~177 responses, ~3,000 pages.
  • June 2026 — Oz previewed the program at ITS America Detroit: 10 states joined at that point, FHWA providing $200K per participating state, an "open-source backend" for interoperability (GovCIO interview).
  • Oct 9, 2026 — DCI Coalition Data Collection, Quality, and Validation Working Group workshop (Anchor States + industry), defining minimum data requirements for interstate work-zone data (CMU Safety21).
  • Nov 13, 2026 — $20M GADC NOFO announced: public data feeds along multi-state corridors; four priority areas — work zones, congestion, freight bottlenecks, emergency response (USDOT release).

Corridors and anchor states

Ten anchor-state DOTs are publicly reported — California, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Iowa, Kansas, Minnesota, Nevada, Oklahoma, Texas — with Oz's own materials saying 11; the eleventh and the corridor-to-state mapping are not public [unconfirmed]. What is confirmed at corridor level: Nevada DOT leads I-80 (11 states, ~2,900 miles, AtkinsRéalis selected Nov 2025 as lead consultant; NDOT calls it the prototype for I-35, I-10, I-15); an I-35 coalition runs jointly with I-80 under pooled-fund study TPF-1799 (Iowa lead, Nevada and Texas co-champions); New Mexico leads an I-40 SMART grant with UC Berkeley PATH; the Eastern Transportation Coalition covers I-95. Note for us: California is an anchor state — and Fresno County sits on the SR-99 freight spine that connects to two likely corridors (I-5/I-10 reach).

Standards stack and governance

The confirmed technical backbone is WZDx → Connected Work Zones (CWZ), now being formalized as a joint AASHTO/ITE/NEMA standard with SAE involvement; FHWA reports 35 states already publish WZDx/CWZ feeds under EDC-8 "Connected Corridors." Governance runs through the DCI Coalition working groups, the TPF-1799 pooled fund, and OST-R NOFOs. USDOT's Sep 2026 National AV Strategy carries a "Facilitate Interoperable Corridors" objective — DCI's charter inside the AV strategy.

2The gap nobody has claimed: location referencing

Every structural gap our research found converges on the same missing layer — and no USDOT or DCI document addresses it:

  • Each state's linear referencing system is incompatible with every other's. ARNOLD/HPMS requires each state to keep an LRS, but routes and measures differ state to state; cross-state conflation is "computationally heavy, error-prone, and hard to verify." AASHTO's own data committee lists "inconsistencies in location referencing" as a named gap.
  • WZDx ships raw GeoJSON coordinates (plus optional mileposts) with no entity-ID slot — so every consumer (Waze, Google, each OEM, each neighboring state) re-map-matches every feed against its own basemap, per feed, per vendor.
  • The one live AV→agency feed proves the demand and the problem at once: Waymo began sharing pothole detections with city and state DOTs in April 2026 — through Waze for Cities, as lat/lon points on Waze's proprietary map (Waymo blog).
  • The previous neutral-ID attempt, SharedStreets, is dormant (no releases since ~2021–23). Its lesson: a referencing system without a funded, versioned, continuously published reference dataset dies. Overture supplies exactly what SharedStreets lacked — and is already working with OGC to make GERS a community standard.

The one-sentence positioning

DCI has states agreeing on a message format (CWZ/WZDx) but not on a shared way to say where — GERS is a free, versioned, globally consistent entity reference that lets every agency and every fleet keep its own schema and its own authoritative data, and still join on one key.

The freight frame: aviation already runs this way

Every airline departure is planned around weather and NOTAMs on a shared national picture. Trucking has no equivalent: a container leaving the Port of Long Beach for the heartland crosses a dozen jurisdictions whose closures, work zones, and restrictions live in incompatible systems. The demo shows the fix at Midwest scale — four states' live events on one keyed network, good enough for pre-trip routing around construction the way dispatch routes around a storm. This is the version of DCI that speaks directly to USDOT's freight agenda, and it is achievable near-term: the feeds exist today, GERS exists today, and the conflation is demonstrably a solved problem (99% auto-match, 3.4 m). What remains is adoption, not invention.

4What Overture should propose: the GERS-Referenced Observation Profile

A thin, additive profile — not a new standard — that any producer (state DOT, AV fleet, work-zone device vendor) attaches to an existing message. Five elements:

1 · The reference

gers_id (UUID) + overture_release (e.g. 2026-11-18.0) + feature type, with optional lane index and linear offset along the segment for lane-level work zones.

2 · Coordinates stay primary

GeoJSON geometry remains required, so non-GERS consumers lose nothing — mirroring DATEX II's multi-method pattern and WZDx's existing coordinates + milepost + name parallelism.

3 · Match provenance

Who performed the GERS match (producer vs. aggregator), method, and confidence — so consumers can weigh trust.

4 · Churn handling

Segment ID churn is ~0.7–0.8%/monthly release; producers refresh against the GERS registry/changelog each release, consumers resolve splits via changelog.

5 · The missing bridge file

No state-LRS↔GERS bridge exists today. A one-time conciliation table per state (Caltrans postmiles ↔ GERS IDs) is the enabling artifact — and a deliverable Overture is uniquely positioned to produce.

What travels over it — both directions

Fleet → agency (the OEM/commercial standard to propose). One GERS-keyed feed any OEM, AV operator, or commercial fleet can publish, carrying whatever observation classes they are willing to share, each an aggregate keyed to a segment ID rather than a trace of any vehicle:

  • Lane-level usage — vehicle counts per lane per time bucket ("rides per lane"), the ground truth states cannot see today without pavement sensors.
  • Traffic patterns — speed profiles and travel-time reliability by segment, time of day, and day of week.
  • Hard-braking and harsh-event clusters — the leading indicator of near-misses; a hard-braking hotspot on a GERS segment is a safety signal years before the crash data catches up.
  • Road condition — pothole/damage detections (the Waymo–Waze precedent, made portable), signage changes, faded markings.
  • Work-zone confirmations — cone patterns, lane shifts, workers present, cross-validating the state feed.

Agency → fleet (the return channel). The same reference carries authoritative restrictions outward: approved heavy-vehicle routes, bridge weight limits, oversize/overweight permit networks, seasonal and hazmat restrictions — published per GERS segment, so a routing engine or in-cab system consumes every state's truck network as one dataset and a driver knows where they can legally go before the trip starts. Aggregation thresholds and k-anonymity floors belong in the profile so no class of observation identifies a vehicle or a driver.

From shared data to shared operations

Aviation did not stop at a shared weather picture; it built flow management on top — ground holds, reroutes, slot allocation. The same ladder applies here, and every rung runs on the same GERS-keyed layers:

  1. Shared picture (the demo, today): every state's events, one keyed network, consumed by anyone.
  2. Coordinated response. States and corridor coalitions subscribe to national event layers by segment set — "notify me when anything touches my stretch of I-80 or the corridors feeding it." A blizzard closing I-80 in Wyoming triggers automated asks downstream: hold planned construction on the detour routes, surge debris crews, keep the alternate corridor at full capacity — construction paused out of economic need to move freight. Today that coordination is phone calls between TMCs; keyed subscriptions make it an event-driven system.
  3. Demand-aware flow management. Navigation partners let their users opt in to sharing destination and route intent — the highway equivalent of a filed flight plan — aggregated into expected load per segment per hour (consented, aggregated origin–destination flows only; no individual trips). With predicted demand and live events on the same segments, corridor operators can grant time-windowed freight allowances and variable speed limits — faster overnight freight windows, passenger priority at peaks — published as time-bound attributes on GERS segments. Overture's transportation schema already models conditional speed-limit rules, so the data shape exists; the regulatory authority for dynamic limits is state law and belongs on the open-questions list.

Beyond the interstates: the local mile

A crash closes the freeway. Navigation apps instantly pour thousands of vehicles onto local streets — streets whose signal timing assumes an ordinary Tuesday — and today, nobody tells the city. With the incident and its detour load on one keyed feed, the local response becomes systematic:

  • Signals adapt. The city or county TMC subscribes to the segment sets feeding its grid; a freeway closure triggers pre-planned signal retiming along the absorption corridors instead of leaving arterials to drown.
  • Officers deploy. Police and public works are notified with the exact segment list absorbing freeway load and dispatch traffic officers to the intersections that need direction.
  • Costs follow the incident. Each event carries the emergency-services incident ID (CAD/NG911) alongside its GERS references — gers_id says where, the incident ID says why — so officer hours and equipment charge back to the incident, building the cost-recovery trail that reimbursement programs (FHWA Emergency Relief, FEMA PA) require.
  • The feds learn the real emergency routes. The after-action record — which local segments absorbed how much diverted load, how often — shows FHWA where de-facto emergency routes actually run and where traffic-management investment belongs.

This is also why counties and cities belong in the coalition, not only states: the detour always lands on a road a local agency owns.

Venues, in order of leverage

  1. CWZ standards committee (AASHTO/ITE/NEMA) + the WZDx GitHub — propose an optional location_references array with a GERS entry type. Additive, non-breaking, lands in the exact spec DCI is standardizing on.
  2. SENSORIS (ERTICO) — a profile citing "Overture/GERS + release" in the map-identification envelope makes OEM→cloud observations natively GERS-keyed.
  3. OGC community-standard process — fold the observation-profile requirements (lane/offset sub-referencing, release pinning) into the GERS standard while it's being drafted.
  4. GADC award teams — get GERS referencing written into a funded multi-state pilot's data plan (see §6).
  5. AVSC / SAE V2X — longer-term: an AVSC best practice recommending GERS-keyed AV→agency reporting; AVSC is where Waymo, Zoox, Aurora et al. already sit.

The flywheel: standard → Overture ingestion → national analytics

Once agencies and fleets publish GERS-keyed data, the loop closes inside Overture itself — and this is the part to bring home as an Overture proposal, not just an external pitch:

  1. Authoritative ingestion into the transportation theme. The Addresses theme already set the precedent: it is built from openly licensed authoritative government datasets, not OSM. Propose the same pathway for transportation — a formal contribution channel where state DOT data (speed limits, truck networks, functional class, lane counts) and conformant OEM-derived data enter the theme as sources, keyed by the GERS IDs they already carry. Each new authoritative source reduces reliance on OSM-derived attributes and the ODbL share-alike friction that comes with them, and raises the theme toward automotive grade: verified speed limits and, over time, lane-level understanding.
  2. The national needs ledger. With usage (lane volumes), risk (hard-braking clusters), condition (damage detections), and disruption (work-zone/closure history) all keyed to the same national segment set, the federal question "which roads cost us the most?" becomes a query instead of a study. USDOT can rank every segment in the country by cost and need — maintenance burden, safety exposure, freight delay — and direct funding out of national need rather than application quality. That ledger is the deliverable that outlives any one administration, and the reason to move while USDOT is actively asking for it.

5Spec: GERS Digital Corridor Pilot

Problem. States on DCI corridors are standing up work-zone feeds that every consumer must re-map-match, and AV fleets have road observations agencies want but no neutral way to reference them. Without a shared entity reference, DCI ships eleven corridors of coordinate soup and the conflation cost lands on every consumer, forever.

Goals. (1) An Anchor State DOT publishes a WZDx/CWZ feed carrying GERS references in production. (2) At least one AV or fleet-data producer publishes observations keyed to GERS on the same corridor. (3) A GERS location-referencing extension is formally proposed to the CWZ committee. (4) Overture is named data-infrastructure partner in at least one GADC application. Non-goals: replacing any state LRS or schema; building a consumer app; lane-level HD mapping; hosting agency data.

User stories

  • As a state DOT traffic engineer, I want my existing LCS/work-zone records tagged with GERS IDs so neighboring states and OEMs consume my feed without re-conflating it.
  • As an AV fleet operator, I want to report cone patterns and lane shifts against a public, versioned segment ID so one export serves every jurisdiction I operate in.
  • As a corridor coalition analyst, I want state feeds and fleet observations joined on one key so I can see where fleets confirm — or contradict — the official picture.
  • As a county public-works department, I want to attach my permits and assets to the same IDs the state uses so my data participates without a GIS integration project.

6If it lands: the reference architecture

The question that follows the pitch is "who hosts this and where are the endpoints?" The answer that fits DCI's politics — state-led, AASHTO's insistence on state-DOT centrality, Oz's "open-source backend" — is federation with a thin national fabric. Nobody hands their data to anyone. States keep publishing from where they publish today; Overture keeps hosting the reference layer it already hosts; the federal pieces are a registry, an archive, and standards — all of which exist in some form now.

Why this is a small ask

Count the genuinely new components: the location_references field (a spec change), the open-source tagger sidecar (this demo's pipeline, productionized), a resolver API on infrastructure Overture already runs, a registry column, and one subscription service. Everything else — state feeds, federal registry and archive, Overture's releases, RITIS-style shared hosting — exists and keeps its current owner and funding. That is what makes "operational within a single funding cycle" a credible sentence rather than a slide.
